**It can — but better work does not automatically mean better learning. Research increasingly suggests that ChatGPT helps most when students use it to question, practice and get feedback, and helps less when it simply produces the work for them.**

That distinction matters because several different outcomes often get mixed together:

- completing an assignment faster;
- getting a better grade;
- understanding the subject;
- remembering it later;
- applying the knowledge to a new problem.

AI can improve the first two without necessarily improving the others.

In one experiment with **211 students**, the benefits lasted mainly among students who used AI as a partner for inquiry and learning. When students delegated production of the work to the model, the advantage largely disappeared once the AI was removed.

Other studies show a similar pattern: students can produce better essays or code and experience less frustration without demonstrating an equivalent increase in retained knowledge.

At the same time, AI tutoring itself is not inherently harmful. A randomized Harvard study found strong learning gains from a purpose-built AI tutor. But that system was designed around teaching: it guided students through material, controlled the learning path and provided targeted feedback rather than simply delivering answers.

So the important question is not:

**“Did the student use ChatGPT?”**

It is:

**“What part of the thinking did ChatGPT do?”**

| AI use | Likely effect |
| --- | --- |
| Explaining a difficult concept | Can support learning |
| Asking the student questions | Can support active recall |
| Giving feedback on the student’s reasoning | Often useful |
| Generating the full answer | Improves output more reliably than learning |
| Replacing the difficult thinking step | Risks cognitive offloading |

## AI Caver take

Educational AI works best when it removes unnecessary friction without removing the mental effort that creates competence.

The real challenge for universities is therefore not eliminating AI. It is learning how to distinguish **better student work** from **better student understanding**.
